     Strengths (Reasons to Accept):

     Addresses a Critical Real-World Gap: The paper tackles two
     significant and practical limitations of current biometric systems:
     vulnerability to noise and the closed-set assumption. This focus on
     "open-set" scenarios and "noise-resilience" is highly
     relevant for real-world deployment.

     Technically Sound Approach: The proposed two-stage framework (denoising
     + metric learning) is logical and leverages state-of-the-art deep learning
     architectures (WaveNet, ResNet, angular margin losses). The combination is
     a sensible and promising approach to the problem.

     Promising Quantitative Results: The reported performance metrics
     (Precision@1 up to 98%, TAR@1%FAR up to 95%) are impressive and suggest the
     framework is highly effective. The analysis on the benefit of decoupling
     the objectives adds valuable insight.



     Novel Application Domain: Applying this specific, robust framework to
     "brain-derived signals" (EEG) for biometrics is a strong and
     interesting case study, pushing the boundaries of non-traditional
     biometrics.


     Weaknesses (Reasons to Reject):

     Lack of Specificity and Validation (from the Abstract): The
     abstract is a summary, but for a rigorous review, the absence of concrete
     details is a major concern. There is no mention of the dataset(s) used, the
     number of subjects, the type of auditory stimuli, or a comparison against
     existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods. The results, while high, are
     presented in a vacuum.

     Potential for Overclaiming: Terms like "systematically
     addresses" and "robust solution" are strong. Without a
     comparative analysis against other noise-robust or open-set methods, such
     claims cannot be properly evaluated.



     Incremental Contribution Risk: While the combination of WaveNet
     denoising and metric learning for EEG biometrics might be novel, each
     component individually is well-established. The paper's contribution might
     be seen as an engineering application of existing tools rather than a
     fundamental scientific breakthrough, depending on the depth of the
     technical adaptation required.

     Major Issues Addressed: The paper addresses three major, interconnected issues in biometric authentication:

     Noise Robustness: The vulnerability of biometric systems,
     especially those using physiological signals like EEG, to various types of
     environmental and physiological noise.

     Open-Set Recognition: The practical requirement for a system to not only
     identify known individuals but also reliably reject imposters or unknown
     users, moving beyond the simplified closed-set assumption of most academic
     studies.



     Scalability: The need for a system that can gracefully accommodate new
     users without full retraining, which is implied by the open-set and metric
     learning approach.


     Importance of the Issues: These issues are of paramount importance for the field. The vast majority of biometric research
     is conducted in controlled environments under closed-set settings. Translating these successes to real-world, uncontrolled
     conditions is the grand challenge of the field. Therefore, the paper's focus is on a highly significant and impactful set of
     problems.

     Degree of Novelty, Creativity, and Technical Depth:

     Novelty: The novelty appears to lie in the specific combination
     and application of these techniques. Using a WaveNet for denoising raw (or
     pre-processed) EEG signals for the purpose of biometric authentication is a
     creative and well-justified choice. Similarly, applying deep metric
     learning with angular margins to create an embedding space for EEG-based
     identity is a strong methodological fit. The paper's own contribution of
     "explicitly decoupling noise mitigation and embedding learning
     objectives" is a key insight that demonstrates a thoughtful system
     design.

     Creativity: The creativity is evident in framing the biometric problem
     as a two-stage pipeline that mirrors a "signal enhancement then
     discrimination" approach, which is intuitive and effective.



     Technical Depth: The abstract suggests significant technical depth.
     Implementing and optimizing a WaveNet with a scale-invariant SNR objective
     requires expertise. Integrating this with a ResNet backbone and angular
     margin loss (like ArcFace or CosFace) for metric learning is non-trivial.
     The ablation study mentioned ("highlights the importance of explicitly
     decoupling") points to a rigorous experimental methodology. However,
     the true depth can only be assessed by reviewing the full paper, including
     architecture diagrams, training details, and a thorough analysis of failure
     cases.


     Detailed Feedback for Authors (to be included in the review):

     The concept and results are compelling. To strengthen the paper,
     the full manuscript must include a rigorous experimental section. Please
     specify the EEG dataset(s) used, including size, number of subjects, and
     the nature of the auditory stimuli.

     A direct comparison with several strong baseline methods is crucial.
     This should include traditional EEG biometric approaches as well as other
     deep learning-based methods, both for closed-set and open-set
     scenarios.



     Provide more detail on the open-set evaluation protocol. How were
     unknown users simulated? What were the specific FAR/FRR thresholds?



     Elaborate on the "adaptive signal enhancement." How does the
     WaveNet module adapt to different noise types?



     Discuss the computational cost and latency of the two-stage framework,
     as this is critical for real-time authentication applications.
