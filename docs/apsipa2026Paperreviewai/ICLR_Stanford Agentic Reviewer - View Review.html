Multimodal Mamba Fusion for Noise-Robust EEG Biometric Identification

ICLR Submitted: April 28, 2026

Summary

This paper proposes a two-stage EEG biometric pipeline that augments a WaveNet denoiser with a single selective state-space (Mamba) block and adds a parallel spectrogram branch with Mamba sweeps along frequency and time, fusing both embeddings via cross-attention and a learned gate. On the PhysioNet Auditory-Evoked-Potential dataset under a held-out-subject open-set protocol (for verification) and session-disjoint closed-set retrieval (for identification), the full multimodal model (V4) achieves the best Precision@1 across three synthetic noise families, while the open-set verification on held-out subjects remains near chance (AUROC ≈ 0.5), which the authors acknowledge.

Strengths

  • Technical novelty and innovation
    • Introduces a hybrid Mamba-augmented WaveNet denoiser and a spectrogram branch with Mamba sweeps, combined via cross-attention with a learned gating mechanism.
    • Applies selective state-space modeling to both time-domain denoising and time-frequency embedding within a unified biometric pipeline.
  • Experimental rigor and validation
    • Reports metrics across multiple random seeds for core variants (V1, V2, V4) and three noise families, and includes standard deviations.
    • Measures both signal reconstruction quality (SI-SNR) and downstream retrieval/verification metrics, with a transparent acknowledgment of verification shortcomings.
  • Clarity of presentation
    • Architecture and training details are described clearly (layer choices, STFT parameters, loss functions, auxiliary losses, sampler).
    • Figures and tables align with the described design and provide an intelligible flow from denoising to embedding.
  • Significance of contributions
    • Addresses a relevant challenge for EEG biometrics: robustness to noise and cross-session/held-out-subject generalization.
    • Opens a line of inquiry into multimodal fusion (raw waveform + spectrogram) for EEG identification, with practical runtime considerations.

Weaknesses

  • Technical limitations or concerns
    • The held-out-subject verification performance (AUROC ≈ 0.5) indicates the learned embedding is not open-set discriminative; this severely limits real-world biometric utility.
    • The ablation across V1/V2/V4 retrains Stage I independently for each version; the large SI-SNR differences across runs confound attribution of retrieval gains to Stage II (spectrogram/fusion) versus better denoising.
    • Only a single Mamba layer is inserted in Stage I; the rationale for its placement and its limited effect is not deeply examined (e.g., multiple Mamba blocks, alternative integration points).
  • Experimental gaps or methodological issues
    • Missing comparisons against strong, commonly used EEG backbones (EEGNet, ShallowConvNet/DeepConvNet, ResNet1D, TCNs) and spectrogram-based baselines; results are only relative to the authors’ prior pipeline.
    • No subject-disjoint identification experiment (i.e., identification on unseen subjects), which is often considered a core biometric scenario; the “closed-set retrieval” remains easier and less informative than a subject-disjoint identification test.
    • Fusion ablations (e.g., concatenation vs cross-attention, removal of the gate, varying auxiliary loss weights, spectrogram-only branch) are absent, limiting insight into which components are essential.
    • Verification setup lacks critical details (e.g., enrollment protocol, number of enrollment samples per subject, prototype construction), and no calibration or low-FAR operating-point analysis is provided.
    • Noise is fully synthetic at evaluation; robustness to realistic artifacts or cross-session/device/channel variance is not assessed.
  • Clarity or presentation issues
    • The interplay between Stage I retraining and Stage II improvements is not isolated; Tables mix outcomes across differently trained denoisers, making attribution ambiguous.
    • The quick-run V3 on different hardware adds little and distracts from the controlled comparisons.
  • Missing related work or comparisons
    • Does not engage with large-scale, multi-session EEG biometric studies such as the PEERS benchmark (e.g., Fallahi et al., 2025), which provide more realistic open-set and permanence analyses.
    • Limited discussion of recent compact CNNs and attention/SSM-based EEG decoders, and how they might serve as baselines or complementary approaches.

Detailed Comments

  • Technical soundness evaluation
    • The selective state-space modeling and multimodal fusion are reasonable design choices. However, the insertion of a single Mamba block mid-WaveNet is a minimal modification; given no gains in P@1 for V2, it is unclear whether the state-space substitution is actually beneficial without further exploration (e.g., stacking, tuning state dimension, placement).
    • The cross-attention fusion over a length-2 token sequence is simple and computationally light; empirical evidence that it outperforms naive concatenation or weighted averaging would strengthen the claim of its necessity.
    • The two-stage training is straightforward, but given the downstream objective is verification/identification, end-to-end fine-tuning (or partial unfreezing of Stage I) might improve discriminability and should be assessed.
  • Experimental evaluation assessment
    • The core internal comparison (V1/V2/V4) indicates small to moderate P@1 gains primarily in power-line and EMG conditions, with minimal gains for Gaussian noise. Yet SI-SNR for V4’s Stage I is markedly higher than in V1/V2, confounding interpretation because Stage II relies on the outputs of different denoisers. A fair ablation would fix Stage I weights across versions or hold SI-SNR constant while varying Stage II architecture.
    • The absence of baseline models widely used in EEG decoding/biometrics (e.g., EEGNet, ShallowConvNet/DeepConvNet, ResNet1D with metric learning) reduces the external validity of the claims; without them, it is hard to gauge whether the proposed design is competitive beyond the authors’ own pipeline.
    • Open-set verification is the critical metric for biometrics; AUROC ≈ 0.5 and EER ≈ 0.35–0.41 suggest the current embedding is not usable for verification. While candidly reported, this undermines the central motivation and indicates a need for calibration strategies (score normalization, AS-Norm, cohort normalization), specialized open-set losses, or prototype modeling.
    • The evaluation uses synthetic noise only. Given EEG artifact diversity (EOG/EMG bursts, motion, electrode pops), testing on real noisy segments or cross-session/device robustness would better support noise-robustness claims.
  • Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
    • Large-scale studies (e.g., PEERS; Fallahi et al., 2025) show deep metric learning can achieve moderate EERs with careful protocols and emphasize permanence and session/device effects. The present work uses a much smaller dataset (N=20) and does not demonstrate improvements in the more challenging open-set verification regime, leaving open whether the proposed architecture would scale or translate.
    • EEG classification backbones like EEG-DCNet and standard EEGNet/ShallowConvNet families provide competitive baselines and design ideas (multi-scale temporal filters, attention) that could be contrasted or combined with the proposed Mamba blocks.
    • Prior denoising and autoencoding work (e.g., DCAE with time/frequency loss) suggests spectral constraints can aid reconstruction. The spectrogram Mamba branch resembles this intuition but is used for embedding; ablations that connect SI-SNR/spectral reconstruction quality to identification metrics would be enlightening.
  • Discussion of broader impact and significance
    • EEG biometrics raise privacy and security concerns; the paper’s frank reporting of weak open-set verification is important for responsible claims. A discussion of threat models (beyond zero-effort impostors), potential spoofing via stimulation replay, and fairness (across demographics, devices) is missing.
    • From a practical standpoint, the reported runtime is promising, but without robust verification performance, deployment is premature. The proposed modular design could still be of value as a research scaffold for future calibration- and robustness-focused work.

Questions for Authors

  1. How exactly are enrollment prototypes constructed for verification (number of enrollment samples per subject, session selection, averaging or other aggregation)? Please provide a detailed description and report ROC/DET at low FARs (e.g., 1%, 0.1%).
  2. Can you provide a fairer ablation that fixes Stage I denoiser weights across V1/V2/V4 (or uses a shared denoiser checkpoint) to isolate the effect of the spectrogram branch and fusion on retrieval and verification?
  3. What are the results of simpler fusion strategies (concatenation, weighted sum without attention) and a spectrogram-only branch? Also, how sensitive are results to the auxiliary loss weights (λe, λs) and to removing the learned gate?
  4. Did you attempt end-to-end fine-tuning (unfreezing parts of Stage I) or explicit verification-oriented calibration (e.g., score normalization, cohort-based normalization, margin tuning)? If so, how did AUROC/EER change?
  5. How do the models perform under a subject-disjoint identification protocol (queries from unseen subjects), and how does that compare with your closed-set retrieval results?
  6. Beyond synthetic noise, have you evaluated on real artifact-contaminated sessions or across days/devices/channels to substantiate noise robustness and generalization?
  7. Can you elaborate on why V4’s Stage I SI-SNR is substantially higher than V1/V2 if architectures are nominally similar? Were hyperparameters or training schedules different, or could this reflect instability that should be controlled?

Overall Assessment

Estimated Score: 4.9/10 (Calibrated to ICLR scale)

The paper tackles an important and timely problem—noise-robust EEG biometrics under open-set conditions—and presents a thoughtfully engineered architecture that combines Mamba-based sequence modeling in both time and time-frequency domains with cross-attention fusion. The writing is clear and the experimental reporting is honest. However, the experimental evidence is not yet sufficient for a top-tier venue: open-set verification performance is near chance, ablations are confounded by retraining different denoisers per version, and comparisons are restricted to the authors’ own baselines without reference to standard EEG models or subject-disjoint identification settings. While the approach is promising and the modular design could serve as a useful research baseline, stronger methodological controls, broader baselines, and meaningful verification performance (with proper calibration) are needed to support publication at ICLR. I recommend rejection in the current form, with encouragement to address the above issues.
